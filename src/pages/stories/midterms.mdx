After the US midterms ended with a historic blue wave, a different battle ignited on X between pollsters and prediction markets.

Tarek Mansour, CEO of Kalshi, [declared victory for 'the markets'](https://x.com/mansourtarek_/status/2118137937707913265) by citing his platform's higher accuracy in forecasting House races.

He also framed the 'win' as a triumph of 'the people' over elites who cannot "see and feel what the everyday man, on the ground, sees and feels."

Veteran forecaster Nate Silver [swiftly fired back](https://x.com/NateSilver538/status/2118151715719401663) by calling Kalshi's victory 'stolen valor'. "The better election models were more confident about the outcome than the markets and got there more quickly," he wrote.

The truth sits somewhere in the middle.

While Mansour was right to claim that Kalshi was more accurate than many polls in predicting midterm House results, his platform lagged behind both [Nate Silver's FLIPR model](https://www.natesilver.net/p/nate-silver-2026-midterm-election-senate-model) and Polymarket when it comes to Senate outcomes.

On the night before the election, Kalshi was not the most accurate forecaster for any of the 33 Senate races. When placed head-to-head against its archrival Polymarket, it was more accurate in just 12 races compared to Polymarket's 19.

Although it did better in forecasting gubernatorial and House races, Kalshi nevertheless lagged behind leading pollsters when it came to predicting the outcome with confidence before the polls opened.

This outcome puts a serious dent in the claim that prediction markets are unassailable 'truth machines'. [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-05/kalshi-and-polymarket-get-a-passing-grade-on-the-us-midterm-elections) gave the platforms a 'passing grade' for their midterm forecasting.

It also challenges some of the longstanding assumptions about prediction markets.

[Previous studies](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5331995) of such markets suggested that accuracy is created by liquidity: the higher the relative volume of transactions, the more likely they are to produce a more accurate result.

However, Kalshi failed to decisively outpredict Polymarket, despite capturing approximately 84.5% of the total trading volume in Senate races. The difference of 422 million shares seems to have made very little impact on its forecasting ability.

In Minnesota's Senate race, it trailed Polymarket in forecasting by around 2.5–3 percentage points despite having 10 times the volume of shares.

The exact reasons for this discrepancy are up for debate. Joachim Klement, a managing director for investment bank Panmure Liberum, [wrote in the Financial Times](https://www.ft.com/content/d41df5d6-bde3-4887-a233-5c0f26a7defd) that 'whales' could shift the odds by betting on their 'preferred outcome'.

As a result, the markets reflect the views of a few 'wealthy punters' rather than a crowdsourced range of opinions, thus turning the 'truth machine' into an op-ed.

Domer, a prominent geopolitics trader from Las Vegas, blamed the results on an excess of emotion from politically biased users.

“There was a lot of resistance to just accepting the reality that Texas was going to go blue,” he told Bloomberg.

Users of either Kalshi or Polymarket are unlikely to be fazed, but the results should call into question their widespread use as a data source by journalists, analysts and the finance industry. Bloomberg, CNN and the Wall Street Journal offer prediction market odds--but perhaps it's time to take a closer look at their limitations.
