Chuqi Chen, a journalism student, had five years’ worth of savings from her production company and a steady stream of rental income from her Beijing flat.

The problem was, she couldn’t find a landlord to take her. The hunt for student digs began in July. By August, one month from the start of school term, she was starting to panic.

“Some of the landlords took three to five working days to reply. Two of the flats I was keen on, they simply vanished. They didn’t even bother to send me a video,” she recalled.

“By 15 August, I was thinking about Airbnb but that would leave me with no permanent address to ship my stuff,” she added.

Neptune Wharf, a new build-to-rent apartment in Surrey Quays, was initially considered too expensive at £2000 per month. However, as the search for housing grew more and more desperate, she decided to bite the bullet.

It was a decision she does not regret. The neighbourhood was quiet and close to her university. The unit’s high windows—overlooking Deptford Park—made the room feel spacious. The occasional maintenance issues were handled with military promptness.

When the water heating broke one morning, she didn’t need to call her landlord or scrape Reddit for a “reliable Deptford plumber”—Neptune’s in-house team fixed it in less than two hours.

## Renting, as it should be?

<Figure size="wide" caption="An billboard by Uncle, one of London’s fastest-growing build-to-rent operators.">
	<StoryImage src={uncleAd} alt="An Uncle billboard reading “Taking the ‘Lord’ out of landlord”" />
</Figure>

Across London, apartments like Neptune Wharf are quickly rising up in defiance of financial gravity. They are known as [‘build-to-rent’](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/67aafe8f3b41f783cca46251/NPPF_December_2024.pdf) (BTR)—purpose-built housing that is typically 100% rented out and professionally managed under a single owner.

Most are owned by institutional investors like Blackstone or Lone Star Capital, with day-to-day running outsourced to property companies like Greystar or Galliard.

The pitch to wannabe tenants is simple: no mould, no leaky pipes, no ‘dodgy’ landlords. For the right price, you can live like an influencer with all the perks of a five-star hotel. As one ad from UNCLE put it, this is ‘Renting, as it should be’.

<Figure caption="Thomas Wilson arrived at Neptune Wharf after working for Lendlease and Argent.">
	<StoryImage src={thom} alt="Thomas Wilson, building manager, sitting in a Neptune Wharf kitchen" />
</Figure>

Thomas Wilson, Neptune Wharf’s building manager, compares his role to that of a ‘foster father’ for Neptune Wharf’s 250-odd tenants. He greets every new arrival at the door, offering them a tour of the apartment’s fixtures and a Lonely Planet guide to Deptford.

“You’re coming in jetlagged, tired and carrying 15 boxes. I want a quick easy journey for you. My idea is for someone to come in and be settled immediately,” he explained.

“Where’s the Tesco? Where are the shops? Where’s the train station? I even had a message from someone asking me what train to take from Piccadilly,” he added.

In his experience, all property is an emotional decision and BTR is no different. Tenants need to imagine living there from the moment they step in. Hence, the branded appliances (“Smeg”), the plush common area (“£1 million kit-out”) and the private cinema (“110 bookings in one month”)

	<StoryImage src={commonRoom} alt="Neptune Wharf’s common room, with curved white shelving around a round table" />

	<StoryImage src={sundeck} alt="Bloom East’s covered rooftop terrace with deckchairs and string lights" />

However, in BTR world, Thom’s sales pitch is relatively modest. At Bloom East in Nine Elms, tenants enjoy the services of a pet spa complete with two showers, bathtub and a drying station. Its neighbour, Moda Living, offers video games on demand, inside a games room the size of a small lecture hall.

“We had a tenant who wanted to play Football Manager 2026, so we bought it on the PS5 for him,” said Babur, the building’s duty manager.

Such perks do not come cheap. Neptune Wharf’s monthly rent is about [40% higher than the Lewisham average](https://www.ons.gov.uk/visualisations/housingpriceslocal/E09000023/#rent_price) for a one-bedroom flat (£1,429) but this hasn’t affected its bottom line. Thom has just 2 empty units to offer, and zero arrears in rent.

“Usually, everyone’s paid and if it’s late, it’s typically 1 or 2 days,” Thom said.

## Promise & Peril

<Figure size="wide" caption="Southall, located on the Elizabeth line, has seen a BTR boom.">
	<StoryImage src={southall} alt="Looking up at new build-to-rent towers and cranes in Southall" />
</Figure>

Upper middle-class expats are not the only ones seduced by pet spas and personalised welcomes. BTR’s biggest fan is arguably the UK government, which sees it as an answer to London’s housing crisis.

Since 2018, the [National Planning Policy Framework (NPPF)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/67aafe8f3b41f783cca46251/NPPF_December_2024.pdf) has recognised BTR as a distinct category—in order to “simplify its treatment within the planning system”.

The tax regime is equally favourable, as Thom notes. Billionaire investors like Sable Capital, which owns Neptune Wharf, pay a 17% stamp duty land tax, the same rate as private landlords owning a second property above £1 million.

London’s private rental market was also ripe for disruption. Ownership is fragmented and the levels of professionalism are low. Most landlords own just one or two properties, and most treat it as a ‘second job’.

Thom puts the problem more bluntly: “That person doesn’t know a plumber, or maybe the manager doesn’t know who to call for a broken window.”

All of this has fuelled a booming industry. In 2025, an estimated [30% of London’s new homes are build-to-rent](https://www.landlordzone.co.uk/news/build-to-rent-savills-thirty-percent), with more in the pipeline.

Bloom’s 900-unit Nine Elms project will soon be dwarfed by its 1,624-unit “Pearl Yard Bermondsey”. The new flagship project is built on a disused biscuit factory and costs up to £600 million.

In Southall, existing build-to-rent towers by Grainger and UNCLE will be joined by a £1 billion investment from developer Montreaux Homes. It will transform the old Maypole margarine factory into “Margarine Works”, with over [1,216 new BTR homes](https://ealing.news/news/planning-permission-application-made-for-600m-development-of-maypole-margarine-factory-site-in-southall/) alongside a boutique hotel.

Even [John Lewis has entered the industry](https://www.johnlewispartnership.co.uk/our-company/our-businesses/build-to-rent), promising “to alleviate some of the growing pressures on the UK’s housing market” with “a brand people know and trust, and a stand-out commitment to service.”

For local councils, the BTR boom is irresistible. Housing is scarce and budgets are tight. Through a Section 106 agreement, they can grant planning permission to billionaire developers in exchange for public services—most often in the form much-needed affordable housing.

Adam Almeida, a data analyst from the [Commonwealth Think Tank](https://www.common-wealth.org/author/adam-almeida), is highly sceptical of such trade-offs. Not least because redevelopment immediately causes a gentrification effect that renders affordable housing standards meaningless.

“Wherever there is a build-to-rent property, there is a widespread increase in rent on existing properties. This increase is borough-wide, regardless of the size,” he explained over the phone.

His research on Brent is damning. Although the borough added more than 5,000 homes in London’s biggest ever BTR project at Wembley, rental prices in the borough continue to trend upwards in defiance of supply.

Adam also believes the urgency of London’s housing crisis undermines councils in their negotiations with developers. London’s affordable housing requirement was initially set at [50](https://www.london.gov.uk/programmes-strategies/planning/implementing-london-plan/london-plan-guidance/affordable-housing-and-viability-supplementary-planning-guidance-spg)%, before being [watered down to 35%, then to 20%](https://committees.parliament.uk/committee/17/housing-communities-and-local-government-committee/news/210107/housing-committee-raises-concerns-on-governments-london-housing-delivery-affordable-homes-plan/).

However, even this 20% figure has become out of reach for many developments. In Lewisham, [Landsec’s £1.5billion redevelopment](https://www.insidehousing.co.uk/home/plans-approved-for-2800-homes-on-lewisham-shopping-centre-94310) of Lewisham Shopping Centre will offer 1,700 BTR homes, 660 student beds and 445 ‘co-living homes’.

The affordable housing floor space totals [just 15%](https://www.selondoner.co.uk/news/lewisham/05112025-lewisham-councillor-defends-redevelopment-plans-amid-criticism-over-affordable-housing), with only 98 beds in the most urgent category of social housing.

“There is very little stick to discipline private capital into delivering what is legislation and to follow regulation. Even the most paltry of deliveries doesn’t pan out,” Adam said.

[Professor Dan Durrant](https://profiles.ucl.ac.uk/33795-dan-durrant/publications) from UCL Bartlett School of Planning, who wrote the most-cited study of BTR, concurs. Although he believes BTR has a ‘small marginal role’ in housing Londoners, it was never going to be a model that would truly ‘fix’ the problem.

<Figure size="column">
	<Embed src="https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/BGUVQ/5/" title="Social rent v. market rent" height={905} />
</Figure>

“Those affordable housing policy requirements at 50%. That’s always an aspiration. You were never going to get that,” he told me candidly over a Zoom interview.

Professor Durrant is also concerned that even those low affordable housing thresholds cannot be properly enforced.

“What if someone on affordable rent loses their job? What if they move out? You could see the affordable housing component falling over time if there’s no oversight,” he added.

He has found data from Sheffield showing that 50% of small environmental measures were planned but never delivered. In Southwark, campaigners like 35percent are [already calling out the local council](https://www.35percent.org/posts/southwark-abandon-annual-affordable-housing-monitoring-reports/) for failing to stand up to its BTR landlords. Their audit has turned up missing data and large amounts of ‘uncategorised housing’, with no explanation or follow-up.

“My scepticism comes from the fact it’s well known that local authorities have very little enforcement and monitoring for planning conditions,” Prof. Durrant said.

“When we asked policymakers about how this would be managed over time, they didn’t really seem to have very clear answers,”

## Up and up

<Figure size="wide" caption="Moda Living in Nine Elms opened in July 2025 and boasts a 100% occupancy rate.">
	<StoryImage src={modaLiving} alt="A Moda Living tower framed by the opening at the top of a dark stairwell" />
</Figure>

In 2023, FT journalist John Burn-Murdoch [wrote a column titled ‘Repeat after me: building any new homes reduces costs for all’.](https://www.ft.com/content/86836af4-6b52-49e8-a8f0-8aec6181dbc5)

Lamenting the ‘exasperating’ sceptics who ignore basic supply and demand, he suggests that “building unsubsidised housing pushes down rents and prices while freeing up cheaper properties”.

When I presented Adam Almeida with this argument, he exploded in a fit of exasperation: “Their response is 100% in line with trickle-down economics. Which is, we are going to provide more homes for the top tier of the renting class and then we’re going to decrease [housing] demand for everyone else.”

In his view, BTR is a Faustian bargain. It looks good on paper for local governments but is ultimately self-defeating in the long term.

“ My gripe is not even with the private sector. My gripe is with the public sector. Like we know, like they’re demonstrating time and time again that they will not fulfil the job of providing affordable housing for the general public. They are only interested in and have only provided for a very small and privileged subset of the renting class,” he added.

Meanwhile, BTR continues its seemingly inevitable ascent across London’s skyline. [Transport for London is partnering with Grainger](https://www.connectedlivinglondon.co.uk/arnos-grove/) for BTR projects in Oval, Finchley, Southall and Nine Elms. Five minutes from Chuqi’s window, Lendlease is joining forces with UNCLE to build another 251 apartments at Deptford Landing. In [his 2025 Plan](https://www.london.gov.uk/media-centre/mayors-press-release/towards-new-london-plan#:~:text=In%20a%20major%20speech%2C%20the,on%20families%20and%20younger%20Londoners.), Mayor Sadiq Khan praised the model and spoke of how London may deliver more affordable homes through “the growing build-to-rent model, purpose-built student accommodation and other specialist housing.”

Whether this bargain will create new homes or cost Londoners dear, nobody really knows. For now, it’s one of the few solutions in a city short on housing and ideas. Local governments lack the funds to build what they need, and private capital wants to build as they wish. There will be a struggle will be played out in the three-hour-long committee meetings, filled with boos and jeers, and picket signs.

Perhaps it is Thom, the building manager, who summarises it most succinctly : “A key thing that people forget about property is: To you, it’s personal. To me, it’s business.”
